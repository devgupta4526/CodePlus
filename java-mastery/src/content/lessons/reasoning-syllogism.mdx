# 8. Syllogism & Deductive Logic

---

## 1. What is Syllogism?

**Syllogism** is a form of logical deduction consisting of a set of premise statements followed by conclusions. Your goal is to determine which conclusions logically and undeniably follow from the given statements, assuming the statements are absolutely true (even if they defy real-world facts, e.g., "All dogs are cats").

---

## 2. Standard Types of Statements

We use **Venn Diagrams** to visualize the relationships. There are four primary categorical propositions:

1. **Universal Affirmative (All A are B)**
   - **Diagram**: Circle A is completely inside Circle B.
   - **Meaning**: Every element of A belongs to B. However, 'All B are A' is not necessarily true.

2. **Universal Negative (No A is B)**
   - **Diagram**: Circle A and Circle B are separate, with a strict crossing line (-) indicating no interaction.
   - **Meaning**: Zero overlap. 'No B is A' is also absolutely true.

3. **Particular Affirmative (Some A are B)**
   - **Diagram**: Circle A and Circle B intersect/overlap partially.
   - **Meaning**: At least one element is common. 'Some B are A' is also true.

4. **Particular Negative (Some A are not B)**
   - **Diagram**: A specific dotted region in A has a crossing line (-) connecting to B.
   - **Meaning**: There is at least a portion of A that will never touch B.

---

## 3. The "Possibility" Concept

A conclusion stating something "is a possibility" is true **only if**:
1. It is logically feasible based on the diagram.
2. It does not directly contradict any original statement.
- *Rule of Thumb*: If a relationship is completely unknown (no direct link in the diagram), any possibility regarding them is **True**.

---

## 4. The "Either/Or" Case (Complementary Pairs)

An 'Either/Or' condition occurs when you have two conclusions that contradict each other, but one of them *must* mathematically be true. 
**Conditions for Either/Or:**
1. Both conclusions must be individually **False** (Doubtful).
2. The subject and predicate (the two entities) must be exactly the **same** in both conclusions.
3. They must form a complementary pair:
   - "Some A are B" AND "No A is B"
   - "All A are B" AND "Some A are not B"
   - "Some A are B" AND "Some A are not B"
